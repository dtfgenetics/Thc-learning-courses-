# Module 4 — Traceability, Material Movement, Inventory & Waste

## Why this module matters

A cultivation operation can only trust its crop records if it knows **which material the record belongs to**. A perfect environmental reading, treatment record, harvest note or lab sample has limited value if plant or batch identity has been lost.

GS1’s traceability model is useful because it is technology-neutral: identify the object, capture events, preserve location/time/source/destination relationships, and make the history reconstructable. That approach fits a cultivation operation whether the employer uses paper records, spreadsheets, an ERP, a state-mandated seed-to-sale platform, or a combination of systems.

ASTM D8556-25 also confirms that cannabis/hemp operations can be managed through formal quality-management systems, but the course does not reproduce paywalled clauses or pretend a voluntary ASTM practice overrides jurisdictional law.

## Lesson 4.1 — Identity through the cultivation workflow

### Identity is operational infrastructure

A cultivation operation may track individual plants, groups, lots, batches, rooms, containers, samples or other controlled objects depending on its process and jurisdiction. The naming system can vary, but the purpose is the same: keep observations and actions connected to the correct material.

GS1 distinguishes class-, batch/lot- and instance-level identification. Technician I does not need to memorize GS1 identifiers; the educational value is the underlying decision: **what level of identity is required for this material and event?**

### Transitions create identity risk

Identity is easiest to lose when something changes:

- seed or clone becomes a plant unit;
- transplant changes container or location;
- plants move between rooms;
- material is divided or combined;
- a sample is taken;
- harvest material changes container;
- waste is separated from usable material;
- data is moved from paper to digital systems.

Before a transition, verify the physical identifier against the work order and destination. After the transition, verify that the controlled record matches physical reality.

### Genealogy matters

If a new identifier replaces or extends an earlier one, preserve the relationship. A harvest container does not erase the source plant/batch history. A sample ID should still connect to the material it represents. Good genealogy lets the operation reconstruct what happened and prevents unrelated records from becoming attached to the wrong material.

### Worked case

A plant tag does not match the move order. The plant resembles the others and is probably part of the same cultivar group. Appearance is not enough to overwrite controlled identity. Stop the affected movement and resolve the mismatch under procedure.

### Guided practice — genealogy chain

Trace a mock workflow from donor plant to clone tray, transplant batch, flowering room, harvest container and retained sample. For each transition, record:

- source identity;
- new/continuing identity;
- location;
- time/date;
- quantity/count where relevant;
- person/system recording the event.

### Retrieval check

1. Why is cultivar name alone usually insufficient as a unique identity?
2. At what points in cultivation is identity most vulnerable?
3. Why should a replacement identifier remain linked to the prior one?

## Lesson 4.2 — Movement, inventory and vendor-neutral tracking

### The record should follow physical reality

A movement entry should correspond to a real, authorized physical event. A useful generic movement record includes the identity of what moved, source, destination, time, applicable count/quantity, and the responsible person or system.

The exact legal data fields vary by jurisdiction. This course therefore teaches the stable operational logic rather than pretending one software interface is universal.

### Reconciliation is comparison, not forced agreement

Inventory reconciliation compares the physical state with the controlled record. If the system expects 48 plants and the physical count is 47, do not change either side simply to make the numbers agree. Recount and verify within procedure, preserve the discrepancy, and escalate if it cannot be resolved.

A discrepancy can indicate:

- counting error;
- delayed record entry;
- wrong identifier;
- unrecorded movement;
- loss/damage;
- software or synchronization issue;
- another process failure.

Technician I gathers permitted facts; a supervisor/compliance/QA role may own the investigation or final correction.

### Worked case

A room move is complete physically, but the digital platform is unavailable. The site has a controlled downtime procedure. Record the event using the approved contingency method so the true time and identity are preserved, then reconcile when the system returns. Do not wait until the end of the day and reconstruct multiple movements from memory if the procedure provides a better method.

### Guided practice — movement packet

Complete two simulated movements:

1. a normal movement with matching identity/count;
2. a movement containing a physical-versus-record discrepancy.

For the second, document what you verified, what remained uncertain, what you did **not** change, and who was notified.

### Retrieval check

1. What is the difference between reconciliation and changing a record until it matches expectations?
2. Which movement facts are stable across software platforms?
3. Why is a controlled downtime process better than retrospective memory entry?

## Lesson 4.3 — Discrepancies, waste and destruction documentation

### Waste does not automatically lose identity

Plant material designated as waste can still require identity, quantity, status and disposition records. The exact destruction method and reporting rules are jurisdiction-specific and must be taught locally. Technician I should understand a safer general principle: **do not destroy the evidence chain before the required record is complete**.

### Preserve the discrepancy before correcting it

When the physical material and record disagree, preserve enough information to reconstruct what was observed. Silent corrections can make an investigation impossible. WHO data-integrity guidance supports maintaining complete, accurate, traceable records and preserving change history.

### Hold questionable material when required

If waste lacks an identifier, a destruction entry exists while material remains, or two batches are about to be combined before required records are complete, stop the uncontrolled transition. The appropriate role determines final disposition.

### Worked case

The digital system shows a batch as destroyed, but the physical waste container is still present. Throwing it away would make the record appear correct while concealing the discrepancy. Preserve the material/status, report the conflict, and follow the reconciliation process.

### Guided practice — discrepancy investigation

Learners receive a mock event history with:

- beginning count;
- one transfer;
- one plant removed from production;
- one delayed digital entry;
- final physical count;
- one mislabeled waste container.

Build the most defensible timeline, identify what can be confirmed, identify what remains uncertain, and write the escalation note.

### Module application — traceability reconciliation packet

Produce a portfolio-ready simulated packet containing:

- genealogy diagram;
- movement record;
- inventory reconciliation;
- discrepancy note;
- waste/disposition handoff.

### Module retrieval

Explain the relationship among **identity**, **event history**, **physical verification**, **reconciliation**, and **disposition**. Then explain why a tracking platform can support traceability but cannot replace disciplined technician behavior.
