# Module 2 — Sanitation, Biosecurity & Controlled Movement

## Why this module matters

A cultivation facility can spread a biological problem faster than it discovers it. Workers, tools, carts, hose ends, containers, incoming plants, media, water and plant debris can move pests or pathogens. A technician who understands pathways helps protect the crop; a technician who treats sanitation as cosmetic cleaning can unintentionally connect every room into one contamination route.

**Controlled references:** `REF-UMASS-GREENHOUSE-SANITATION`, `REF-PSU-GREENHOUSE-DISEASE-SOURCES`, `REF-EPA-WPS-APPLICATION-RESTRICTIONS`.

## Lesson 2.1 — Contamination routes and zone discipline

### Think in pathways, not appearances

Biosecurity is easier to understand when you ask, “How could material move from this place to that place?” Pathogens or pests do not need to ride on a visibly sick plant. Greenhouse disease guidance identifies contaminated media, tools, hose ends, plant debris, containers and work surfaces as possible sources or routes. Workers and carts can connect areas as well.

Penn State specifically warns that clean media can be recontaminated through dirty tools, hose ends, surfaces and materials. This is an important mental model: **clean status is not permanent if the item re-enters a contaminated pathway**.

### Zone names vary; movement logic does not

Facilities may use terms such as clean zone, production, quarantine, isolation, mother room, propagation, hold area or restricted area. Names vary. The operating principle is stable: movement controls reduce the chance that a worker or object carries risk from a higher-risk area into a cleaner or more sensitive area.

When the SOP uses clean-to-dirty movement:

- perform lower-risk work first;
- follow clothing/glove/footwear change rules;
- keep room-specific tools in the assigned zone where required;
- sanitize shared equipment before crossing boundaries;
- avoid unnecessary backward travel.

### Worked case

A cart used in quarantine is urgently needed in propagation. Wiping one visible spot is not automatically sufficient. The cart follows the approved cleaning/disinfection and release workflow before entering the cleaner zone. Production urgency does not remove the contamination pathway.

### Guided practice — contamination map

On a facility map, draw arrows showing possible movement through:

- technician travel;
- shared tools;
- carts;
- irrigation hose ends;
- incoming plant material;
- waste/debris removal.

Then redesign the sequence to reduce avoidable cross-zone travel.

### Retrieval check

1. Name five non-plant contamination routes.
2. Why can a healthy-looking incoming plant still require quarantine?
3. What is the purpose of clean-to-dirty sequencing?

## Lesson 2.2 — Cleaning, disinfection and sanitation verification

### Cleaning and disinfection are different operations

UMass greenhouse guidance distinguishes physical cleaning from disinfection. Cleaning removes weeds, soil, media, organic matter and debris. Disinfection uses an approved product/process to reduce targeted microorganisms on appropriate cleaned surfaces or equipment.

Organic debris matters because it can prevent disinfectant contact or reduce efficacy. A bench covered with roots, growing media and leaf debris is not ready for a simple spray-and-walk-away sanitation step.

### Follow the approved product and method

Disinfectants are not interchangeable. Products differ in labeled use, concentration, contact conditions, hazards and material compatibility. Follow the current label and facility SOP. Never strengthen a solution because “more must be better,” mix products unless specifically allowed, or invent an unlabeled use.

A generic sanitation sequence for learning purposes is:

1. secure/remove plant or material as the procedure requires;
2. remove debris and gross soil;
3. physically clean the surface or item;
4. verify the approved product and preparation;
5. apply as directed;
6. maintain required contact/wet conditions where applicable;
7. complete the defined rinse/dry/finish step;
8. inspect the result;
9. document completion and exceptions.

The exact method comes from the product label, equipment constraints and facility SOP.

### Worked case

A worker reaches for a sanitizer from the same brand family as the SOP product, but its active ingredient and label differ. Stop and verify the approved product. Brand similarity is not evidence of interchangeable use.

### Guided practice — sequence and error hunt

Learners receive sanitation-step cards plus incorrect actions such as “apply over heavy debris,” “double concentration for safety,” “skip label check” and “combine unknown cleaners.” Put valid steps in order and remove unsupported actions. Explain whether each error primarily threatens efficacy, worker safety, material compatibility or record integrity.

### Retrieval check

1. Why should gross organic material generally be removed before disinfection?
2. What information controls concentration/contact conditions?
3. Why is “looks clean” not proof that a controlled sanitation process was completed?

## Lesson 2.3 — Quarantine, holds and biosecurity breach response

### A hold preserves decision space

Quarantine or hold status protects the operation while an unresolved condition is evaluated. The concern might involve suspected pest/disease risk, incoming material, pesticide entry restrictions, identity uncertainty or another controlled issue. Technician I does not independently release a hold because plants look healthy.

Do not confuse plant-health quarantine with pesticide restricted-entry status. They can coexist, but they come from different control systems.

### Stop further spread first

If a quarantine tray appears in general production, a tool crosses a controlled boundary without required sanitation, or an area is entered improperly, the first objective is to stop additional movement or exposure within the procedure. Preserve identity and the current state. Notify the correct role and document what is known.

Useful breach information includes:

- exact area/material identifiers;
- time discovered;
- movement that already occurred;
- tools/carts/people potentially involved;
- immediate control taken;
- person notified;
- remaining uncertainty.

### Do not destroy evidence while “fixing” the problem

Moving questionable material without documenting its route, discarding labels, combining lots, or cleaning every involved object before the movement history is captured can make the situation harder to investigate.

### Decision scenario

A tray from quarantine is found on a general-production cart. The plants look healthy. Returning it quietly would erase part of the exposure history. A stronger response is to stop further movement, preserve identity, follow the breach procedure, identify where the cart traveled, and escalate for the authorized containment/release decision.

### Module application — biosecurity breach packet

Complete a simulated breach report containing:

- room map;
- plant/tray identifiers;
- worker/cart movements;
- sanitation status;
- immediate controls;
- open questions;
- required handoff.
