# Instructor Guide — COURSE-LH-TECH1-001

## Instructional purpose

Teach entry cultivation technicians to execute assigned work safely, protect crop identity and biosecurity, use controlled instructions, document work accurately, perform only authorized operator-level equipment care, and escalate conditions outside their authority.

Course 1 should be taught as **workplace decision training**, not as a vocabulary lecture. Learners should repeatedly explain what they observed, which rule controls the decision, what action is inside their authority, what must be documented, and when responsibility transfers to another role.

## Teaching model

Use a repeating cycle:

1. short evidence-backed explanation;
2. worked example;
3. guided practice;
4. learner-generated artifact or decision record;
5. retrieval;
6. workplace scenario with changed surface details;
7. feedback tied to the controlled objective;
8. equivalent reassessment when remediation is needed.

Avoid lecture-only delivery and avoid teaching learners to memorize answer wording. Revisit earlier material throughout the course: safety boundaries recur during equipment work; biosecurity recurs during movement; controlled-document authority recurs during deviations; traceability recurs during records and handoff; data integrity recurs in every written artifact.

## Instructor preparation

Before delivery:

- verify that examples do not conflict with the current employer SOP, product label, equipment manual, emergency plan, pesticide restrictions, or jurisdiction-specific cannabis rules;
- replace mock forms with approved site forms when training is workplace-specific;
- verify that local examples preserve the same Course 1 objective rather than inserting an unstated local rule into a generic assessment;
- review active visuals and accessibility equivalents;
- prepare at least one alternate scenario for each objective likely to require remediation;
- distinguish instructor experience from controlled evidence whenever adding an example.

Do not present the course as granting pesticide-handler, respirator, electrical, HVAC, hazardous-energy/LOTO-authorized, supervisory, medical-diagnostic, or platform-specific certification.

## Module facilitation

### Module 1 — Applied workplace safety

Emphasize hazard recognition, changing-condition reassessment, task-specific PPE/HazCom use, stop-work/escalation, occupational respiratory/allergy awareness, and the distinction between recognizing a fault and being authorized to repair it.

Use room-entry, slip/electrical, alarm, treated-area, changing-condition, and work-related symptom-pattern cases. Require learners to state **what changed**, **whether exposure remains controlled**, and **what action is inside their authority**.

Common coaching error: rewarding a learner who identifies the hazard but then proposes unauthorized repair, diagnosis, or chemical work.

### Module 2 — Sanitation and biosecurity

Teach contamination pathways before chemical products. Learners should map movement of people, tools, carts, hose ends, incoming plants, debris, containers, and waste.

Reinforce:

- source → route/carrier → boundary → receiving zone reasoning;
- cleaning before disinfection where the approved process requires it;
- protection from recontamination after sanitation;
- current label/SOP control;
- quarantine, hold, isolation, and pesticide restricted-entry as separate status systems;
- release authority.

Common coaching error: treating a completed sanitation step as permanent protection regardless of what touches the item afterward.

### Module 3 — SOPs, work orders, authority and escalation

Require learners to work from current controlled instructions. Practice identifying revision/status, scope, prerequisites, required sequence, acceptance criteria, documentation, exceptions, alternate escalation paths, and truthful incomplete-work status.

Never reward improvisation that exceeds authorization. If documents conflict, learners should preserve the conflict and use the controlled resolution path rather than blending revisions.

Common coaching error: accepting "I know how to do it" as evidence that the worker is authorized to act.

### Module 4 — Traceability, inventory and waste

Teach identity and event history independent of any one vendor platform. Practice:

- traceable object identity;
- source/input → event → output relationship;
- source/destination/time/quantity or count where applicable;
- split and merge genealogy;
- physical-versus-record reconciliation;
- discrepancy containment;
- waste/disposition lineage;
- preserving uncertainty when evidence conflicts.

A learner must never force a record or physical condition to match expectation simply to close a workflow.

Common coaching error: treating reconciliation as a requirement to make two numbers agree.

### Module 5 — Equipment readiness, operator care and fault reporting

Use pre-use inspections, baseline-versus-abnormal comparisons, out-of-service decisions, stored-energy cues, servicing-boundary exercises, and recurring-fault timelines.

Draw a bright line between operator care and servicing. A strong technician can recognize when **not** to open, bypass, reset repeatedly, clear, reach into, diagnose, or repair equipment.

Fault reporting should separate observation from hypothesis and capture asset/location, timing, recurrence, permitted checks, process impact, safe action, and escalation.

Common coaching error: praising a plausible root-cause guess when the objective is disciplined observation and escalation.

### Module 6 — Records, shift handoff and integrated workflow

Apply data-integrity principles as a transferable record-quality model while keeping their regulatory source boundary explicit. Require factual separation among observation, event time, entry time where relevant, action, result, unresolved condition, correction history, and hypothesis.

Use two-way handoff practice: outgoing preparation, prioritized exchange, incoming questioning/cross-check, and explicit confirmation of critical open items.

The final scenario should integrate safety, identity, equipment, biosecurity, controlled instructions, records, and changing conditions rather than testing each domain in isolation.

Common coaching error: accepting a handoff because an issue was mentioned even though status, ownership, next action, or read-back remains unclear.

## Assessment use

Each module currently has a **14-item formative test** with immediate rationale-based feedback, for **84 formative items total**. These tests support retrieval and remediation; they are not the certification exam.

The 36-item final course test is summative at course level. The integrated practical is required because knowledge questions alone cannot demonstrate safe workflow execution. Current numeric thresholds remain developmental until pilot review, calibration, and standard setting are complete.

The current 84/36/120 public-item counts describe the present bank. They are not ceilings. Additional valid items may be added, replaced, retired, or reorganized as the course evolves.

## Remediation model

Remediation must address the **objective and reasoning error**, not just the missed answer.

### Step 1 — Identify the gap

Use item metadata, learner explanation, workbook evidence, or practical observation to identify the controlled objective. Ask the learner to explain the original decision before reteaching so the instructor can distinguish a conceptual misunderstanding from a reading error, documentation problem, local-rule confusion, or accessibility barrier.

### Step 2 — Diagnose the misconception

Use `OBJECTIVE-REMEDIATION-MATRIX.md`. Record the actual misconception or performance gap instead of writing only "needs review."

### Step 3 — Assign the smallest useful practice

Return the learner to the specific lesson section, visual, workbook activity, job aid, or exemplar that addresses the gap. Require active work: explain a decision, reconstruct a pathway, correct a record, produce a fault timeline, map genealogy, or complete another artifact.

Passive rereading alone is not sufficient evidence of remediation.

### Step 4 — Coach with questions before answers

Use prompts such as:

- "What fact are you relying on?"
- "Which rule or controlled source governs this step?"
- "What changed?"
- "What is inside your authority?"
- "What remains unknown?"
- "What evidence would the next qualified person need?"
- "What could become irreversible if you continue now?"

Avoid revealing the keyed answer before the learner has reconstructed the decision rule.

### Step 5 — Reassess with an equivalent case

Use `SCENARIO-VARIANTS.md` or an equivalent instructor-created case. Change material surface details so success depends on transfer rather than memory. Do not use the identical formative item immediately after coaching.

### Step 6 — Record evidence

Use `REMEDIATION-RECORD-TEMPLATE.md`. Successful remediation means the learner can apply the objective in a changed context, not merely state that they reviewed the lesson.

## Practical critical errors

For a practical critical error:

1. stop the simulation when continuing would reward or normalize unsafe/falsified conduct;
2. preserve the evidence of what occurred;
3. identify the competency/objective involved;
4. explain the safety, identity, authorization, or record-integrity boundary;
5. assign targeted retraining;
6. reassess using an equivalent form/scenario rather than resuming after revealing the correct response;
7. record prompts used and reassessment evidence.

The development threshold does not authorize instructors to waive a critical-error rule or convert a failed safety boundary into partial credit without the controlled assessment process.

## Using response exemplars

`RESPONSE-EXEMPLARS.md` should be used for critique and comparison, not copying. Useful approaches include:

- show the weak response first and ask what is missing;
- remove one critical field from a strong response and ask what becomes unreconstructable;
- compare two partially correct responses and ask which is more defensible;
- change identifiers/timing/context before asking the learner to produce their own version;
- require the learner to justify each field in a record.

Do not use the public exemplars verbatim as secure credential-exam content.

## Accessibility and remediation

Provide text equivalents for visuals, keyboard-operable digital interactions, readable tables, sufficient contrast, captions/transcripts for audio/video, and an accessible alternative when a visual-only presentation is not itself the competency being measured. Do not use color alone to encode status.

Before labeling a learner response as a knowledge deficit, consider whether the difficulty came from inaccessible presentation, ambiguous instructions, language complexity unrelated to the construct, or an accommodation issue. Remediation must not be used to conceal an accessibility defect in the course itself.

Accommodations should preserve the competency rather than lower it.

## Evidence boundaries

Government, standards, university-extension, and transferred quality-system sources are used only within the scope recorded in the evidence dossier. Instructor experience can enrich examples but must not override controlled evidence or create unsupported universal cultivation rules.

When adding a site-specific example, explicitly separate:

- the stable Course 1 principle;
- the employer's local SOP/process;
- jurisdiction-specific legal requirements where applicable.

## Completion evidence

Retain, as applicable:

- module-test results;
- objective-level performance patterns;
- workbook/challenge-lab artifacts;
- practical rubric and critical-error records;
- final course-test result;
- remediation records and equivalent reassessment evidence;
- instructor/assessor prompts used where they affect interpretation;
- version IDs of the instructional package and assessment objects used.

Completion evidence should show what the learner demonstrated, not merely that a page was viewed or an attempt was submitted.
