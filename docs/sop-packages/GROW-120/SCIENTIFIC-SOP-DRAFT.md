# THC-GROW-120 — Flowering stage management
**Version:** 0.1.0 draft  
**Status:** Controlled draft — review required  
**Release:** Blocked

## Purpose

Define flowering transition, photoperiod verification, reproductive scouting, environment/load monitoring and late-flower risk controls without universal flowering recipes.

## Scope

**Included:** transition readiness; photoperiod verification; flower development; sex/intersex scouting; environmental load; humidity/disease risk; maturity tracking; handoff to harvest.

**Not universalized or not authorized by this draft:** universal flowering duration; universal PPFD/DLI/VPD setpoints; assumption that one photoperiod response fits all genotypes; ignoring intersex findings because plants were feminized; harvest release based on calendar date alone.

## Scientific and operational control principles

### 1. Cannabis flowering development is shaped by genotype, photoperiod and environment, so flowering-stage management should preserve actual developmental observations alongside programmed schedules.
**Evidence boundary:** A programmed light schedule does not prove identical developmental timing across genotypes.
**Sources:** `REF-LIGHT-001`, `REF-FLOWER-MORPH-2023-001`

### 2. Photoperiod and uninterrupted dark-period control are important flowering variables, supporting verification of actual timer/controller operation and light-leak or schedule deviations.
**Evidence boundary:** The course should not convert one research treatment into a universal flowering schedule for all cannabis genetics.
**Sources:** `REF-LIGHT-001`

### 3. Unexpected male or intersex reproductive structures can occur in cannabis and may have genetic, hormonal, developmental and environmental contributors, so reproductive scouting and escalation should continue during flowering.
**Evidence boundary:** No single stress trigger should be treated as a universal explanation for all intersex expression.
**Sources:** `REF-SEX-2026-001`, `REF-PLANT-BIO-001`

### 4. As canopy and inflorescence biomass increase, moisture and heat loads and within-canopy gradients can change, requiring representative environmental monitoring rather than reliance on controller averages alone.
**Evidence boundary:** Research temperature/RH/VPD values are evidence of relationships, not universal flowering setpoints.
**Sources:** `REF-VPD-002`, `REF-FLOWER-MORPH-2023-001`

### 5. Trichome and inflorescence maturation are asynchronous and genotype-dependent, so late-flower tracking should combine representative observations with the defined harvest objective instead of using one visual rule.
**Evidence boundary:** Trichome color or flowering week alone does not directly establish cannabinoid concentration or whole-lot readiness.
**Sources:** `REF-FLOWER-001`

### 6. Late-flower management should preserve pest/disease, reproductive, environmental and irrigation deviations because quality and harvest decisions depend on the crop's actual history, not only its final appearance.
**Evidence boundary:** A visually mature crop does not automatically clear an unresolved plant-health or quality hold.
**Sources:** `REF-FLOWER-001`, `REF-VPD-002`

## Measurement, observation and decision quality

Distinguish planned/target values from actual observations and measurements. Preserve method/instrument context, stage, cultivar/lot, location, timing and relevant environmental/root-zone/process context. Do not turn one study treatment, visual cue or facility example into a universal cannabis specification.

## Records

The applicable form must preserve identity, date/time, location, stage/process, source procedure/version, required condition, actual condition, method/instrument when relevant, action, affected scope, deviation/escalation and release/approval state.

## Deviations

Contain immediate risk within authority, preserve the original evidence, define affected scope, notify the responsible role, and document follow-up. Never backfill a record to make execution appear compliant.
