# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — IPM Scouting

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-IPM-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Operational  
**Primary competency:** `COMP-IPM-001`  
**Prerequisite content:** `MOD-IPM-001`, `LESSON-IPM-SCOUT-002`, `LESSON-IPM-BIOSEC-003`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I should be able to scout crops in a repeatable way, document what is present, recognize common pest/disease/damage indicators at an appropriate entry level, map where the problem occurs, and escalate according to the supplied IPM procedure.

The technician is expected to:
- follow a consistent scouting route
- inspect representative canopy levels and plant surfaces
- check high-risk areas and traps when assigned
- distinguish plant symptoms from direct signs of an organism
- record incidence and simple severity information
- map spatial pattern
- compare repeated scouting rounds
- photograph/sample only according to procedure
- protect biosecurity while scouting
- escalate suspicious or increasing findings without independently making unauthorized pesticide decisions

Technician I scouting should generate evidence that an IPM lead or specialist can act on.

## Core job concepts

### Symptom
A plant response such as yellowing, necrosis, distortion, wilting or spotting. Symptoms can have multiple causes.

### Sign
Direct evidence of an organism or causal agent, such as visible insects, eggs, webbing, fungal structures or other identifiable material.

### Incidence
How many plants/sites show the defined problem.

### Severity
How intense the damage/symptom is on an affected plant or area.

### Spatial pattern
Where the issue appears: random, clustered, edge-associated, intake-associated, irrigation-line-associated, cultivar-specific or room-wide.

## Routine scouting workflow

### 1. Prepare without spreading problems

Before entry:
- review room/IPM status
- use assigned clean tools/PPE
- know the scouting route
- prepare forms/device/trap map
- follow clean-to-dirty route rules

### 2. Use a repeatable route

Inspect representative locations such as:
- room edges
- center
- doors/intakes
- drains/wet areas
- propagation/high-risk interfaces
- multiple canopy heights
- leaf tops and undersides
- stems/nodes/growing tips
- media surface/root-zone area where relevant

A consistent route makes one scouting round comparable to another.

### 3. Observe before naming the cause

Record:
- exact plant tissue affected
- symptom/sign
- number of plants/sites
- severity category
- distribution
- developmental stage
- relevant environment/history

Avoid writing only a pest name without the evidence used to support it.

### 4. Inspect traps when assigned

For sticky cards or monitoring devices:
- confirm trap ID/location
- count/classify only within training
- note trend versus previous round
- replace/reset according to procedure
- preserve the record

### 5. Map the pattern

Examples:
- concentrated near intake
- only one irrigation line
- clustered around one bench
- only incoming clones
- random throughout room
- increasing across repeated routes

Pattern often tells more than one close-up photo.

### 6. Track change over time

A one-time mild observation may become significant if incidence rises each round.

Technician should preserve:
- previous count
- current count
- severity trend
- newly affected zones
- response/intervention date when supplied

### 7. Escalate correctly

Escalation may be triggered by:
- direct sign of regulated/serious pest
- rapid increase in incidence
- new room/zone affected
- unexpected disease-like spread
- quarantine breach
- severe crop damage
- finding outside technician identification confidence

The technician should not apply a pesticide or biological control outside authorization just because a pest seems obvious.

## Worked example 1 — Damage near an intake

**Observation:** Similar leaf damage is concentrated on plants nearest an intake.

**Weak response:** assume every plant in the room has the same problem and recommend room-wide treatment.

**Better response:** map the edge/intake-associated pattern, inspect nearby traps/signs and escalate the localized pattern.

## Worked example 2 — Increasing incidence

Round 1: 2 plants affected.  
Round 2: 6 plants affected.  
Round 3: 12 plants affected.

Even if each plant has mild symptoms, the expanding incidence is meaningful and should be escalated.

## Worked example 3 — Symptom without sign

Leaves show stippling, but no organism is confirmed.

**Weak record:** “Spider mites confirmed.”

**Better record:** “Fine stippling on 8/30 sampled plants in Zone A; no direct organism confirmed in field inspection; photos attached; IPM lead notified.”

## Worked example 4 — Direct sign

Visible insects/eggs/webbing are found and match the technician's trained recognition category.

Record the direct sign, location, incidence and supplied identification confidence rather than only the downstream plant symptom.

## Worked example 5 — One close-up photo

A dramatic close-up is not automatically representative of the entire room. Pair the image with room sampling/pattern data.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- scouting only after damage is obvious
- taking the same route inconsistently
- inspecting only the top of the canopy
- failing to look at leaf undersides
- naming a pest/disease without evidence
- confusing symptom with sign
- recording severity but not incidence
- recording incidence but not location/pattern
- using a close-up as evidence of room-wide condition
- carrying suspect leaves/tools through clean rooms
- failing to compare with prior rounds
- making unauthorized treatment decisions

## Guided practice 1 — Sign or symptom

Classify supplied examples as:
- symptom
- direct sign
- mechanical damage
- insufficient evidence

Explain what extra evidence would strengthen identification.

## Guided practice 2 — Scouting route

Design a route through a room map that covers:
- edges
- center
- intake/door
- irrigation zones
- canopy levels
- traps

## Guided practice 3 — Incidence and severity

Given 20 plant observations, calculate simple incidence and assign supplied severity categories.

## Guided practice 4 — Pattern map

Plot findings on a room diagram and classify the pattern as clustered, edge-associated, line-associated, random or widespread.

## Guided practice 5 — Trend over time

Compare four scouting rounds and identify whether the condition is stable, expanding or resolving.

## Decision scenarios

1. Mild symptoms increasing each round: escalate trend.
2. One severe plant only: document exact location and inspect surrounding plants.
3. Direct pest sign near intake: preserve evidence and map local pattern.
4. Symptom but no direct sign: do not overstate diagnosis.
5. Suspicious plant in clean room: limit unnecessary movement and escalate.
6. Trap count suddenly rises: verify trap ID/count and report change.
7. Problem found only on incoming clones: preserve quarantine boundary.
8. Coworker suggests spraying immediately: treatment requires authorized IPM procedure.
9. Close-up photo shared with no room data: collect representative scouting context.
10. Problem appears along one irrigation line: document the spatial association for diagnosis.

## Portfolio artifact

**IPM Scouting and Trend Report** containing at least three simulated scouting rounds so the learner must interpret change over time rather than one snapshot.

## Completion standard

The learner can execute a repeatable IPM scouting route, create defensible observations, distinguish symptoms from direct signs, quantify and map basic crop findings, compare trends and escalate appropriately without stepping outside Technician I treatment authority.
