# Technician II Job-Practice Unit — Root-Zone & Irrigation Troubleshooting

**Task:** `TASK-CULT2-ROOTZONE-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Diagnostic  
**Primary competencies:** `COMP-IRRIGATION-ADV-001`, `COMP-WATER-QUALITY-ADV-001`, `COMP-ROOTZONE-001`, `COMP-NUTRIENT-DIAG-001`  
**Prerequisite:** Technician I Irrigation; `LESSON-IRR-UNIFORMITY-001`, `LESSON-IRR-SCHEDULE-002`, `LESSON-IRR-MASSBAL-003`

## What changes from Technician I

Technician I executes irrigation and reports abnormal measurements. Technician II must determine whether an observed root-zone problem is more consistent with delivery, timing, volume, accumulation, plant demand, measurement quality or another interacting factor.

## Diagnostic evidence streams

Use together:
- irrigation timestamps and planned volume
- representative emitter output
- pressure/filtration/maintenance context
- substrate moisture or water-content trend
- dryback trajectory
- root-zone EC/pH trend
- runoff/drainage measurements when part of the system
- source/fertigation batch history
- plant stage, size and environment
- spatial pattern by line/bench/cultivar

## Troubleshooting workflow

### 1. Define the pattern
Is the problem one plant, one emitter, one line, one bench, one cultivar, one room or multiple rooms?

### 2. Verify delivery
Measure representative emitter output and compare near/far, high/low and affected/unaffected positions. Nominal emitter rating is not proof of current delivery.

### 3. Review timing and demand
Compare irrigation events with lights, environment, plant stage and measured dryback. A copied clock schedule can be wrong even when every event technically ran.

### 4. Review accumulation and concentration
Rising root-zone EC can reflect several mechanisms. Determine whether evidence points toward insufficient water movement, excessive concentration, nonuniform delivery, altered demand, or another process before choosing a correction.

### 5. Verify sensing
Moisture sensors require substrate/install context. Treat an isolated raw value cautiously when it conflicts with other representative evidence.

### 6. Select the smallest discriminating test
Examples: catch-volume test, colocated moisture check, representative EC sample, filter/pressure check, event-history review or batch verification.

## Worked cases

### Case 1 — One line dries faster
Plants on one irrigation line show faster dryback and higher root-zone EC while the rest of the room is stable.

Best next step: verify line delivery/output and local root-zone moisture before changing the whole-room recipe or schedule.

### Case 2 — Room-wide dryback accelerates
Delivery uniformity is good, but dryback increases across the entire room after canopy biomass and light intensity rise.

Interpretation: increased crop demand is plausible. Review schedule/volume against the changed crop/environment rather than assuming emitter failure.

### Case 3 — Sensor says dry, physical checks disagree
One sensor reports rapid dryback while neighboring sensors and representative substrate checks remain stable.

Best next step: verify sensor installation/calibration before increasing irrigation.

### Case 4 — EC rises after a delivery problem
A partially clogged filter reduced output for part of the room. Root-zone EC rose afterward.

Strong response: connect the accumulation pattern to the documented delivery event, verify recovery after correction and avoid diagnosing a nutrient-formulation problem without supporting evidence.

### Case 5 — Same schedule, different cultivar response
Two cultivars under the same schedule show different dryback rates.

Strong response: preserve cultivar and root-system context. Uniform scheduling does not guarantee uniform plant demand.

## Common Technician II mistakes
- diagnosing from one EC value
- adjusting every room from one affected line
- treating dryback percentage as universal
- trusting nominal emitter output
- changing frequency and volume simultaneously before verifying the problem
- ignoring filter/pressure maintenance history
- assuming every high EC event is caused by excessive fertilizer

## Guided practice
1. Calculate distribution variation from a catch-volume data set.
2. Plot a 48-hour dryback curve with irrigation events and identify abnormal transitions.
3. Compare affected and unaffected line data and rank likely causes.
4. Select the most informative next check from five possible tests.
5. Reconstruct an irrigation fault from event, filter and root-zone records.
6. Write a controlled correction/verification plan that changes one major variable at a time.

## Documentation exercise
Produce a **Root-Zone Troubleshooting Report** with room/zone IDs, symptom pattern, irrigation history, delivery test, dryback/EC trend, environment, hypotheses, evidence for/against, next verification and follow-up result.

## Practical mapping
**Technician II Practical C — Fertigation & Root-Zone Troubleshooting** requires the candidate to move from a verified batch to a downstream delivery/root-zone problem and determine whether the issue is batch, delivery, timing, accumulation or measurement related.

## Portfolio artifact
**Irrigation Distribution & Root-Zone Diagnostic Report** with graphed trends and a documented post-correction verification.

## Completion standard
The learner can use representative measurements and temporal/spatial patterns to troubleshoot root-zone and irrigation performance without relying on universal schedules or single-number diagnoses.
