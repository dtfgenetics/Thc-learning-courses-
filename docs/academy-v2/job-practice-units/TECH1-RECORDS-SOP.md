# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — SOP Execution, Records and Shift Handoff

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-RECORDS-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Operational  
**Primary competencies:** `COMP-PRO-SOP-001`, `COMP-PRO-QA-001`  
**Prerequisite content:** `COURSE-PRO-SOP-001`, `LESSON-PRO-SOP-DESIGN-001`, `LESSON-PRO-DEVIATION-001`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I should be able to execute a supplied work instruction or SOP and leave behind an accurate record of what actually happened.

The technician is expected to:
- identify the correct procedure/work order for the assigned task
- confirm required prerequisites before starting
- follow ordered steps without inventing undocumented shortcuts
- record measurements and completion information when the work occurs
- preserve room, plant, tray, batch or lot identity
- document abnormalities/deviations as facts
- distinguish immediate containment from final disposition
- communicate open work and abnormal conditions to the next shift or responsible role
- avoid hiding, rewriting or silently correcting information in a way that destroys record history

The credential does not require the Technician I to design an enterprise quality system. It requires the technician to be a reliable participant in one.

## Why records matter

A cultivation facility cannot manage what it cannot reconstruct.

Records answer questions such as:
- What was done?
- Who did it?
- When and where?
- Which plants/batches were involved?
- What values were measured?
- Which SOP/work order applied?
- Was anything abnormal?
- What immediate action occurred?
- Who was notified?
- What still needs follow-up?

Good documentation protects crop quality, troubleshooting, traceability, handoffs and accountability.

## Reading an SOP or work instruction

Before starting, locate:
- title/task
- version/current status where supplied
- scope
- authorized role
- prerequisites/training
- safety/PPE
- tools/materials
- ordered steps
- acceptance/normal criteria
- required records
- exception/escalation route

### Technician rule

If a procedure says to collect a reading, there should be a defined place to record it. If a procedure gives a normal range, it should also tell the worker what to do when the condition is outside that range or where to find the escalation instruction.

If the instruction is unclear enough that workers could reasonably perform different actions, escalate the ambiguity rather than inventing a local tradition.

## Routine execution workflow

### 1. Confirm the task and identity

Examples:
- room/zone
- work-order number
- batch/lot/plant/tray IDs
- cultivar/source when relevant
- equipment ID
- procedure version

### 2. Confirm prerequisites

Examples:
- authorization/training
- PPE
- clean tools
- calibrated/approved meter status if required
- correct form/log
- correct material

### 3. Execute the ordered procedure

Do not skip a step because:
- “we usually do it this way”
- the previous shift skipped it
- the crop looks healthy
- the form is inconvenient

If conditions prevent correct execution, use the exception/deviation path.

### 4. Record work as it occurs

Good records are:
- specific
- attributable to the task/person/system
- linked to correct identity
- timely
- legible/readable
- factual
- complete enough for another worker to understand

Avoid reconstructing detailed measurements from memory at the end of the day when the system expects contemporaneous entry.

### 5. Record facts before theories

When something goes wrong, capture:
- time
- location
- equipment/process
- observed condition
- relevant IDs
- measurements
- immediate action
- person notified

Do not start by assigning blame or cause.

### 6. Contain immediate risk within procedure

Examples:
- isolate affected material if instructed
- stop an out-of-control process
- protect identity/records
- prevent further movement
- notify authorized role

Containment is not the same as final disposition.

### 7. Complete handoff

If the task or issue remains open, tell the next person:
- what happened
- exact location/identity
- what has already been done
- what remains open
- work-order/deviation ID
- who has been notified
- any immediate monitoring required

## Record quality examples

### Weak entry

> Water was bad. Fixed it.

### Stronger entry

> Veg Room 1, Zone B. Input EC at 09:05 measured 2.8 mS/cm versus supplied work-order range 1.8-2.2. Irrigation was paused per SOP ENV/IRR-04; sample was rechecked with approved meter and result confirmed. Lead notified at 09:12. Work order 184 remains open.

The stronger record preserves identity, measurement, time, action and open status.

## Correcting a record error

The exact correction method depends on the record system.

General principle:
- preserve the original history
- make the correction attributable
- do not erase or overwrite history in a misleading way
- explain the reason when the system/procedure requires it

In an electronic system, use the authorized correction/amendment workflow. In paper systems, follow the defined correction practice.

## Deviation basics for Technician I

A deviation is a departure from an expected process, condition or result.

Technician responsibilities typically include:
1. recognize the abnormal condition
2. protect immediate safety/product/process as authorized
3. document facts
4. preserve affected identity/scope
5. notify the correct role
6. support follow-up with accurate evidence

The technician usually should not decide final root cause or disposition alone.

## Worked example 1 — Out-of-range sensor

**Situation:** A room sensor is outside the range printed on the work instruction.

**Weak response:** change several controls until the number looks right and do not record the excursion.

**Better response:** verify the reading using the supplied procedure, take the authorized routine action if one is defined, document the condition and action, and escalate when the procedure requires it.

## Worked example 2 — Missed sanitation step

**Situation:** A worker discovers that a required sanitation step was not documented and may not have occurred for one batch.

**Weak response:** complete the box afterward as if the step definitely occurred.

**Better response:** document the uncertainty/deviation, identify the connected batch/process, apply authorized containment and notify the responsible role.

## Worked example 3 — Shift handoff

**Weak handoff:**
> Room 2 irrigation acting weird.

**Better handoff:**
> Room 2 Zone C had no flow at 14:20. Valve isolated per procedure; affected plants identified; work order 184 open; supervisor notified; Zone C requires recheck before next scheduled event.

## Worked example 4 — One-room sensor failure

Do not automatically assume facility-wide impact.

Record:
- room
- sensor/equipment ID
- failure period
- connected crops/lots
- backup measurements if available
- immediate action

The affected scope can then expand or narrow based on evidence.

## Worked example 5 — Ambiguous instruction

**Instruction:** “Monitor plants closely and water as needed.”

**Problem:** Different workers may interpret “closely” and “as needed” differently.

**Technician response:** follow any linked criteria if available; otherwise escalate the ambiguity rather than inventing personal thresholds that become unofficial procedure.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- documenting at the end of shift from memory
- entering a diagnosis as fact
- using the wrong plant/batch ID
- leaving out units
- writing “done” with no task/location detail
- backfilling a missed step as though it happened
- deleting an abnormal measurement because a recheck was normal
- correcting records in a way that destroys history
- failing to record who was notified
- assuming containment means the issue is closed
- giving vague shift handoffs

## Guided practice 1 — Repair the bad record

Rewrite:
> Flower was hot. Adjusted AC. Fine now.

The improved record must include:
- room/zone
- time
- measured condition and units
- supplied expected range
- action taken
- recheck/result
- notification/open status

## Guided practice 2 — SOP completeness

Review a short irrigation SOP and identify missing elements:
- no role authorization
- no meter prerequisite
- no record field
- no out-of-range instruction
- vague step wording

Rewrite the weakest step as an observable instruction.

## Guided practice 3 — Fact or theory

Classify each statement:
- “EC measured 3.1 mS/cm at 10:05.”
- “The nutrient mixer failed.”
- “Plants in Zone A were wilted before irrigation.”
- “The cultivar is weak.”
- “Work order 22 was not completed by the scheduled time.”

Separate observed fact from cause hypothesis.

## Guided practice 4 — Scope the event

Given a sensor failure, identify:
- equipment
- room
- time window
- connected crops/batches
- records needed
- immediate containment

Do not expand scope beyond what the evidence supports without a reason.

## Guided practice 5 — Shift handoff

Create a handoff for an unresolved irrigation deviation using a supplied work-order number, measurements and affected zone.

The next shift should be able to continue without asking what happened from the beginning.

## Decision scenarios

### Scenario A — Required field missing from form
Use the approved alternate/escalation route rather than inventing an undocumented private record.

### Scenario B — Wrong ID selected in electronic record
Use the system's authorized correction workflow; do not hide the original error.

### Scenario C — Recheck normal after abnormal first reading
Preserve both readings and the reason/action rather than deleting the abnormal result.

### Scenario D — Procedure unclear
Pause the affected decision and escalate the ambiguity when the action cannot be safely/consistently determined.

### Scenario E — Missed sanitation step
Document and contain connected material/process according to procedure rather than quietly filling the checkbox.

### Scenario F — Equipment failure with known affected window
Record the time, equipment and connected scope before assuming either no effect or facility-wide effect.

### Scenario G — Next shift arrives before issue closes
Provide specific identity, status, action and open follow-up.

### Scenario H — Worker suspects a root cause
Record the hypothesis separately from observed facts unless/until the cause is established.

### Scenario I — Paper record entered incorrectly
Use the defined correction method that preserves history and attribution.

### Scenario J — Work completed but not recorded
Do not fabricate an exact time/measurement from memory. Follow the facility's late-entry/deviation procedure.
