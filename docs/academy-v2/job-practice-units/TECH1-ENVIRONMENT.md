# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — Environmental Monitoring

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-ENV-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Applied  
**Primary competency:** `COMP-ENV-VPD-001`  
**Prerequisite content:** `MOD-ENV-001`, `LESSON-ENV-VPD-001`, `LESSON-ENV-SENSORS-002`, `LESSON-ENV-LEAF-VPD-003`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I should be able to read environmental information, decide whether the measurement is trustworthy enough to use, recognize normal versus abnormal patterns using the supplied crop/SOP context, and escalate conditions that require a higher-level response.

The technician is expected to:
- read temperature and relative-humidity values correctly
- understand why temperature and RH must be interpreted together
- use VPD as an interpretive tool rather than a universal rigid rule
- check whether a sensor location represents the crop
- recognize obvious sensor disagreement or drift
- compare leaf and air temperature when supplied
- review trends around lights-on/off, irrigation and equipment cycling
- record environmental observations and actions
- distinguish a possible measurement problem from a real room condition
- follow supplied ranges/response procedures instead of inventing facility setpoints

Technician I is not expected to redesign HVAC/dehumidification systems or independently establish the facility's environmental strategy.

## Environmental data on the job

Technicians may encounter:
- dry-bulb air temperature
- relative humidity
- VPD
- leaf/surface temperature
- day/night setpoints
- high/low alarms
- sensor history/trend charts
- room average and zone sensors
- equipment status
- irrigation timing

The number matters only if the technician understands where, when and how it was measured.

## Routine monitoring workflow

### 1. Confirm crop context

Before interpreting a number, know:
- room/zone
- developmental stage
- lights-on or lights-off period
- recent irrigation or major work event
- supplied operating range or alert threshold

Do not apply one memorized VPD target to every stage and facility.

### 2. Check sensor relevance

Ask:
- Where is the sensor?
- Is it at a representative canopy location?
- Is it close to a lamp, wall, intake, dehumidifier outlet or wet surface?
- Does it agree reasonably with nearby sensors?
- Is calibration/current-status information available?

A strange value can be either a real crop condition or a measurement problem.

### 3. Read temperature and RH together

Relative humidity changes with temperature. A room can have the same RH at two temperatures but different evaporative demand.

The technician should avoid statements such as:
> RH is 60%, so conditions are good.

Instead:
> Air temperature is 27 C and RH is 60% during the supplied flower-stage day period. VPD/dashboard status is X relative to the facility range.

### 4. Use VPD in context

VPD helps summarize the relationship between temperature and moisture in the air.

Technician use:
- compare current condition with supplied stage/SOP context
- recognize large excursions
- compare air and leaf context when supplied
- use plant response and sensor quality as supporting information

Technician I should not treat VPD as proof of plant water status, nutrient status or stomatal behavior by itself.

### 5. Review trend, not only snapshot

Look at:
- current reading
- recent high/low
- duration outside range
- lights-on/off transition
- irrigation timing
- equipment cycles
- zone differences

A five-minute excursion and a six-hour excursion are not the same event.

### 6. Compare zones/sensors

If one sensor differs substantially:
- check its location
- compare nearby sensors
- check known calibration/status information
- look for local causes such as dense canopy or equipment outlet
- avoid changing the entire room solely from one suspect reading without following procedure

### 7. Record and escalate

Useful environmental record:
- room/zone
- date/time
- air temperature
- RH
- VPD if system uses it
- leaf temperature if supplied
- sensor ID/location when relevant
- supplied acceptable range
- duration/trend note
- action taken
- person notified/open follow-up

## Worked example 1 — One dry-reading sensor

**Situation:** Four room sensors agree closely. One sensor near a dehumidifier outlet reads much drier.

**Weak response:** lower the whole-room dehumidification target immediately.

**Better response:** check the sensor location and comparison/calibration status before treating it as representative of the crop.

## Worked example 2 — Dense-canopy humidity pocket

**Situation:** Wall controller shows acceptable RH, while a canopy sensor remains humid after lights-off.

**Weak response:** ignore the canopy sensor because the wall controller is the main display.

**Better response:** document the local microclimate and escalate according to the environmental/IPM process.

## Worked example 3 — Daily average hides excursion

**Situation:** The daily average temperature is acceptable, but trend data shows a repeated 90-minute high-temperature event after lights-on.

**Weak response:** report that the room stayed on target because the average is normal.

**Better response:** document the repeated transition excursion and duration.

## Worked example 4 — Leaf-air difference

**Situation:** Air temperature is stable, but leaf temperature is several degrees different from expectation.

**Technician response:** verify measurement method/location and record the leaf-air difference. Use supplied procedure to determine whether escalation is required.

## Worked example 5 — Sudden sensor jump

A sensor value changes sharply with no similar change in nearby sensors or plant appearance.

**Response:** suspect possible measurement/sensor issue and verify before making broad environmental changes.

## Worked example 6 — Real room-wide trend

Several independent sensors rise together and equipment status shows a fault.

**Response:** treat the pattern as likely real, follow the supplied immediate response and escalate the equipment/environment event.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- interpreting RH without temperature
- applying one universal VPD target to all plants/stages
- trusting a single sensor automatically
- ignoring sensor location
- reacting to one unusual number before checking trend
- using daily average to hide short but repeated excursions
- confusing room air VPD with direct measurement of plant water status
- changing multiple environmental controls without documenting the original condition
- failing to note how long a condition was out of range

## Guided practice 1 — Sensor-map review

- light/heat source
- intake
- dehumidifier outlet
- wall
- dense canopy
- wet surface

## Guided practice 2 — Dashboard interpretation

- lights-on transition
- lights-off transition
- irrigation time
- repeated excursion
- likely equipment cycling

Write a five-sentence technician handoff.

## Guided practice 3 — Snapshot versus trend

Compare three cases with the same current reading but different duration histories. Decide which should be treated as the greater operational concern under the supplied SOP.

## Guided practice 4 — Sensor disagreement

Five sensors read similarly; one differs strongly. List the checks you would perform before requesting a whole-room setpoint change.

## Decision scenarios

1. One sensor near an outlet differs from the room: verify location/calibration/context.
2. Multiple sensors move together: treat as likely real and follow response procedure.
3. Wall RH normal but canopy RH high: preserve local observation and escalate.
4. Daily average normal but repeated transition spike: document duration/pattern.
5. Sensor calibration overdue: do not present false confidence in the reading.
6. Leaf/air temperatures differ unexpectedly: verify method and document context.
7. Dashboard value is outside range for two minutes: use duration and supplied response rules before overreacting.
8. Room is outside range and plants show rapid stress: record, take authorized response and escalate promptly.
