# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — Propagation

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-PROP-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Operational  
**Primary competency:** `COMP-PROP-001`  
**Prerequisite content:** `MOD-PROP-001`, `LESSON-PROP-001`, `LESSON-PROP-SEED-002`, `LESSON-PROP-CLONE-003`, `LESSON-IPM-BIOSEC-003`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I should be able to execute routine propagation work from an approved work order while protecting sanitation, plant identity, donor/batch traceability, hydration, rooting environment and acclimatization records.

The technician is expected to:
- verify work order, cultivar/batch and donor identity
- prepare a clean propagation workstation
- inspect donor material and reject/flag visibly unsuitable tissue
- sanitize tools between assigned lots or donors
- make and place cuttings consistently according to SOP
- label trays, cells or propagation lots immediately
- maintain assigned moisture and environmental conditions
- observe rooting progress without damaging material
- record failures, contamination, wilting or unusual development
- acclimatize rooted plants according to the supplied transition plan
- preserve counts and traceability through transplant/handoff
- escalate conditions outside procedure rather than inventing a new protocol

Technician I is executing a validated propagation process. Designing media, hormone programs, mother-plant strategy or tissue-culture protocols belongs in higher-level credentials.

## Core job concepts

### Donor quality
Propagation starts with the condition and identity of the donor material. Stressed, contaminated, mislabeled or otherwise unsuitable material can undermine the entire batch.

### Sanitation at fresh wounds
Fresh cutting surfaces are vulnerable. Tool and workstation sanitation reduce avoidable pathogen movement.

### Water-loss control before rooting
Unrooted cuttings can lose water through leaves faster than they can replace it. The rooting environment must reduce excessive water loss without becoming stagnant or chronically saturated.

### Batch identity
Every tray or lot must remain connected to its donor/cultivar and production record from cutting through rooting, acclimatization and handoff.

### Acclimatization
Rooted material should transition gradually from protected propagation conditions to normal production conditions.

## Routine propagation workflow

### 1. Verify before cutting
Confirm:
- work order
- cultivar/donor ID
- target number
- tray/media
- sanitation requirements
- labeling format
- rooting and acclimatization expectations

### 2. Prepare clean workstation
- clean/sanitize work surface
- prepare sanitized tools
- stage labels before cutting
- separate incoming and completed material
- avoid unnecessary traffic through the propagation area

### 3. Evaluate donor material
Use only material that meets supplied acceptance criteria. Flag obvious pest signs, disease-like symptoms, severe stress, mechanical damage or identity uncertainty.

### 4. Take and place cuttings consistently
Follow SOP for:
- cutting location/size
- node placement
- leaf handling
- rooting product if authorized
- media insertion
- tray position

Consistency makes batch performance easier to interpret.

### 5. Label immediately
Do not rely on memory or temporary verbal identification. Label the batch/tray before it can be confused with another lot.

### 6. Maintain rooting environment
Monitor assigned:
- temperature
- humidity
- media moisture
- light exposure
- airflow

The goal is a stable environment appropriate for unrooted material, not maximum humidity or maximum watering.

### 7. Observe rooting and losses
Record:
- number started
- number visibly rooted by check date
- losses
- contamination
- wilt/desiccation
- unusual cultivar-specific timing

Avoid destructive checking outside procedure.

### 8. Acclimatize and hand off
As roots establish, follow the transition plan toward normal production conditions. Confirm final count, identity and destination at handoff/transplant.

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — Donor concern
A donor branch looks stressed and has suspicious stippling.

**Weak response:** take cuttings anyway because the work order requires a target count.

**Better response:** isolate/flag the questionable donor material and escalate before creating a propagation batch from potentially compromised tissue.

### Example 2 — Mixed labels
Two unlabeled trays are placed next to each other after cutting.

The correct response is to stop and resolve identity before continuing. Guessing based on appearance destroys traceability.

### Example 3 — Wilting after transition
Freshly rooted cuttings wilt after a rapid humidity reduction.

Technician I should document the response and follow the supplied acclimatization/escalation procedure rather than assuming rooting failed or making an unapproved environmental program.

### Example 4 — Different rooting speed
One cultivar roots slower than another but remains healthy.

Record genotype/batch-specific timing rather than forcing every lot onto the same calendar.

### Example 5 — Tool sanitation
A blade is used on a suspect donor and is about to be used on a clean mother.

Stop, sanitize/replace the tool according to SOP, and preserve clean-to-dirty workflow.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- cutting before labels are staged
- using visibly questionable donor material without escalation
- reusing tools across lots without sanitation
- overwatering because cuttings look fragile
- assuming high humidity should remain constant after rooting
- moving trays without preserving lot identity
- checking roots destructively or too frequently
- treating cultivar-to-cultivar rooting differences as automatic failure
- changing SOP variables during the batch without documentation/authorization
- failing to reconcile started, rooted, lost and handed-off counts

## Guided practice

1. Sequence a propagation work order from workstation setup through handoff.
2. Review donor images and classify acceptable, reject/hold, or escalate.
3. Label a multi-cultivar tray map with zero ambiguous positions.
4. Given rooting and loss counts, calculate rooting success percentage and identify the batch needing follow-up.
5. Review an acclimatization trend and choose continue, slow transition, or escalate.

## Decision scenarios

1. Donor has visible pest sign: do not propagate until escalated.
2. Label falls off a tray: stop movement and resolve identity from records before proceeding.
3. One tray is drying much faster than others: verify local condition/delivery before changing all trays.
4. Rooted cuttings wilt immediately after dome removal: follow transition procedure and document response.
5. One cultivar roots three days later than the rest: record batch-specific timing; do not assume universal failure.
6. Coworker suggests skipping sanitation between donors to save time: follow sanitation SOP.
7. Media is saturated and cuttings are declining: document and escalate rather than simply adding more water.
8. Work order calls for 50 plants but only 46 acceptable cuttings are available: record actual count and escalate shortage.

## Documentation exercise

Complete a propagation batch record with:
- work order
- donor/cultivar IDs
- date/time
- operator
- tray/media IDs
- number started
- sanitation confirmation
- environment observations
- rooting check dates/counts
- losses/contamination
- acclimatization status
- final handoff count/destination
- deviations/escalation

## Completion standard

The learner can execute a routine propagation work order safely and consistently, protect sanitation and genetic identity, preserve batch records, recognize common problems and escalate appropriately without redesigning the propagation protocol.
