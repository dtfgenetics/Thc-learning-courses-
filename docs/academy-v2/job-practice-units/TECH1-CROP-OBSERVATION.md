# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — Crop Observation and Scouting

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-OBSERVE-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Operational  
**Primary competency:** `COMP-PLANT-BIO-001`  
**Prerequisite content:** `MOD-PLANT-BIO-001`, `LESSON-PLANT-BIO-001`, `LESSON-PLANT-BIO-ANATOMY-002`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I should be able to look at a crop in a repeatable way and create observations that another worker, lead or specialist can use.

The technician is expected to:
- identify major plant structures correctly
- describe developmental stage from visible morphology
- inspect representative plants/areas rather than only obvious problem plants
- distinguish an observation from an unsupported diagnosis
- recognize meaningful differences in vigor, structure, color, posture, tissue condition and reproductive development
- document location, plant/batch identity and time
- capture useful photographs when required
- connect observations with available environmental or work-history context
- escalate abnormalities according to procedure

Technician I is not expected to independently diagnose every disorder. A strong technician creates reliable evidence for diagnosis.

## Why this matters

Cultivation decisions depend on what is actually happening in the crop.

Weak observation:
> The plants look hungry.

Useful observation:
> In Flower Room 2, Zone C, 6 of 20 sampled plants show interveinal yellowing concentrated on older/lower leaves. New growth appears greener. Photos and 08:00 environment readings attached.

The second note does not claim a cause. It gives the next person evidence.

## Structural vocabulary used on the job

Technicians should confidently identify:
- roots/root-zone condition when visible
- main stem
- branch
- node
- internode
- leaf/petiole
- shoot tip/meristem region
- reproductive structures
- inflorescence/flowering region

Using correct terms reduces vague handoffs such as “the part under the top branch looks weird.”

## Routine crop-observation workflow

### 1. Know the purpose of the walk

Examples:
- general crop condition
- irrigation response
- IPM scouting support
- pre-canopy-work inspection
- propagation check
- flowering development check
- post-environmental-event check

The purpose changes what deserves extra attention.

### 2. Confirm area and identity

Before recording observations:
- room/zone
- cultivar/lot/batch if supplied
- plant/tray/bench ID where relevant
- developmental stage
- date/time

Do not rely on memory when identifiers are available.

### 3. Sample systematically

Avoid only looking at the easiest or worst plants.

A simple routine may include:
- room entrance
- center
- corners/edges
- representative irrigation zones
- multiple canopy heights
- known stress/problem locations

The exact sampling plan is facility specific, but the principle is representative observation.

### 4. Observe whole-plant pattern first

Before zooming in, look for:
- uniformity
- posture
- height/architecture differences
- canopy density
- wilting/drooping
- color patterns
- developmental uniformity
- isolated versus widespread abnormalities

A room-level pattern can be more informative than a single leaf.

### 5. Observe structures

Record relevant findings about:
- leaves: color, spots, margins, curling, damage, orientation
- stems/branches: strength, lesions, breakage, spacing
- nodes/internodes: spacing, branching, reproductive development
- roots/media where visible: color, odor, saturation, visible root condition
- flowers/reproductive structures: developmental stage, abnormal morphology, damage

### 6. Separate observation from cause

**Observation:** leaves in one irrigation zone are wilted while the rest of the room is upright.

**Possible causes:** irrigation delivery, root-zone condition, environment, damage, disease or other factors.

Technician I should document the pattern and available measurements before deciding the cause is proven.

### 7. Add useful context

Depending on task, compare:
- recent irrigation event
- room temperature/RH/VPD
- light or equipment issue
- recent transplant/canopy work
- IPM history
- developmental stage
- recent deviation/work order

The technician does not need to solve the entire case. Context makes the observation more useful.

### 8. Photograph correctly

Useful photo records include:
- overall plant/area view
- closer symptom/structure view
- scale/reference when relevant
- clear focus and neutral lighting where possible
- plant/zone identity linked in the record
- date/time

Avoid color filters or edits that can change symptom appearance.

### 9. Escalate meaningful abnormalities

Examples:
- sudden widespread wilt
- unusual pest-like feeding/damage
- suspected disease spread
- unexpected reproductive structures
- substantial zone-to-zone difference
- damage caused by equipment/work
- rapid environmental response
- strong off-odor/root abnormality

Use the facility procedure rather than silently watching a problem progress.

## Worked example 1 — One wilted plant

**Observation:** One plant is severely wilted; neighboring plants on the same bench appear normal.

**Weak response:** “The room needs more water.”

**Better response:** document the plant and location, check nearby plants and available irrigation/environment context, and escalate the isolated abnormality.

**Reasoning:** one-plant and whole-room patterns suggest different investigation paths.

## Worked example 2 — Zone pattern

**Observation:** Plants at one end of the room have longer internodes and lower canopy density.

**Weak response:** “Bad genetics.”

**Better response:** record the spatial pattern, representative plant observations and available environmental/light history.

**Reasoning:** a spatial pattern may connect to environment, light, irrigation, genetics or other factors. The pattern should be preserved as evidence.

## Worked example 3 — Leaf symptom

**Observation:** Older lower leaves show interveinal yellowing.

**Weak response:** immediately enter a nutrient-deficiency diagnosis as fact.

**Better response:** record which leaves, how many plants, location, severity, developmental stage and available root-zone/nutrition measurements; escalate for diagnosis if outside routine expectation.

## Worked example 4 — Development stage

**Observation:** visible reproductive structures are emerging at multiple nodes.

**Weak response:** assign stage only from days since flip.

**Better response:** document visible morphology together with schedule information.

**Reasoning:** development is a biological state, not only a calendar date.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- recording a diagnosis instead of an observation
- photographing symptoms without plant/location identity
- examining only one convenient plant
- failing to record whether a problem is isolated, zonal or room-wide
- using cultivar name as proof of expected morphology
- assigning stage only from chronological age
- ignoring root-zone condition when above-ground vigor changes
- changing plant conditions before documenting the original state
- using vague words such as “bad,” “weak,” or “off” without description

## Guided practice 1 — Observation or diagnosis?

Classify each statement:
- “Lower leaves on 30% of sampled plants are yellow.”
- “The room has nitrogen deficiency.”
- “Zone B plants are 10-15 cm shorter than Zone A.”
- “The cultivar hates high VPD.”
- “Three plants show stem damage at the support clip.”

Rewrite unsupported diagnoses as objective observations.

## Guided practice 2 — Representative room walk

Given a room map with 120 plants, choose 12-20 observation points that sample:
- edges
- center
- irrigation zones
- entrance/exit
- different canopy areas

Explain why your route is more useful than checking only the worst plants.

## Guided practice 3 — Photo record

Given six crop photographs, add:
- room/zone
- plant or bench ID
- stage
- date/time
- objective description
- severity/pattern note

Identify any photograph that is too altered or unclear to support reliable comparison.

## Guided practice 4 — Pattern and context

A zone shows wilt immediately after irrigation while the rest of the room does not.

List the first five pieces of information you would record or check before escalating.

## Decision scenarios

### Scenario A — One abnormal plant
Document and compare nearby plants before assuming room-wide cause.

### Scenario B — Sudden room-wide droop
Record the pattern and check supplied environment/irrigation status immediately; escalate promptly.

### Scenario C — Unexpected reproductive morphology
Photograph/document actual structures and escalate according to crop/genetics procedure rather than relying on seed/cultivar assumptions.

### Scenario D — Root-zone odor
Record plant/zone identity and root-zone observation; avoid spreading material and escalate if abnormal.

### Scenario E — Damage after canopy work
Document where damage occurred and connect it to the work order/shift history instead of hiding or removing evidence.

### Scenario F — Image appears color-shifted
Retake under more neutral/consistent lighting rather than using the altered image as the main symptom record.

### Scenario G — Edge-only pattern
Record the spatial concentration and compare relevant environmental/light/irrigation context.

### Scenario H — Mixed developmental stage
Document the distribution of stages rather than assigning one stage to every plant by calendar date.
