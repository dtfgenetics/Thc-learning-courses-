# Technician I Job-Practice Unit — Workplace Safety

**Task:** `TASK-CULT-SAFETY-001`  
**Target proficiency:** Operational  
**Primary competency:** `COMP-SAFETY-WORK-001`  
**Prerequisite lessons:** `LESSON-SAFETY-HAZARDS-001`, `LESSON-SAFETY-HEAT-PPE-002`, `LESSON-SAFETY-CHEMICAL-003`

## What the technician is responsible for

A Cultivation Technician I is not expected to design the facility safety program. The technician is expected to work inside it correctly.

That means the technician can:
- recognize common cultivation hazards before and during work
- inspect basic tools and work areas before use
- use required PPE correctly
- keep walking and work surfaces reasonably controlled
- avoid improvising around damaged equipment
- recognize heat-stress risk and warning signs
- follow chemical labels, SDS information and facility procedures
- stop, contain or escalate unsafe conditions within the technician's authority
- document hazards and corrective status accurately

The technician's job is not to prove toughness. It is to complete the work without creating an avoidable injury, exposure, fire, electrical, contamination or equipment risk.

## Why this matters

Cultivation combines agriculture, wet work, electricity, sharp tools, ladders, repetitive motion, lifting, high-intensity lighting, environmental equipment, fertilizers, cleaning chemicals and sometimes compressed gases. Many incidents occur when several ordinary hazards overlap.

Examples:
- irrigation water + damaged electrical cord
- ladder + wet floor
- cutting tools + rushed repetitive work
- chemical PPE + high greenhouse heat
- blocked aisle + emergency response

The correct response is therefore not just “wear PPE.” A strong technician notices the work system around the task.

## Tools and materials the technician may encounter

Depending on the facility:
- pruning shears and scissors
- carts and rolling benches
- step ladders
- hoses and irrigation lines
- pumps and electrical equipment
- fans and environmental equipment
- PPE specified by the task
- cleaning and sanitation chemicals
- nutrient concentrates
- labels and SDS documentation
- spill-control supplies
- safety inspection or hazard-report forms

Facility-specific training controls which equipment a Technician I is authorized to operate.

## Routine safe-work workflow

### 1. Read the work instruction

Before starting, determine:
- assigned task
- work area
- required training/authorization
- required PPE
- equipment/tools
- chemical or material requirements
- special precautions
- record or checklist required

If the instruction conflicts with actual conditions, do not silently improvise. Use the supplied escalation path.

### 2. Inspect the work area

Look for conditions that can affect the task:
- wet or slippery floors
- hoses, cords or debris in walkways
- blocked exits or access paths
- damaged electrical equipment
- damaged ladders/tools
- unstable carts or loads
- sharp objects
- unusually hot or humid conditions
- signs of a leak or spill
- missing guards or barriers

The goal is not to perform a formal engineering inspection. The goal is to avoid stepping directly into an obvious preventable hazard.

### 3. Inspect task equipment

Check that equipment appears fit for use under facility procedure.

Examples of conditions that should stop routine use:
- frayed electrical cord
- damaged plug
- cracked ladder or faulty lock
- broken handle
- missing guard
- leaking chemical container
- unreadable required label
- PPE visibly damaged beyond acceptable condition

Do not repair equipment with improvised tape, wire, zip ties or other temporary methods unless the facility has an authorized repair procedure and the technician is trained to perform it.

### 4. Use the required controls

Think in layers:
1. remove the hazard when possible
2. isolate or redesign around the hazard
3. follow safe procedure and work practice
4. use required PPE

PPE matters, but PPE does not make a damaged electrical cord safe or remove a hose from a walkway.

### 5. Perform the task without creating a new hazard

Examples:
- keep tools controlled when moving between plants
- avoid placing sharp tools in pockets or unstable locations
- do not leave hoses where they create a trip hazard
- manage spills promptly according to procedure
- maintain required aisle access
- do not bypass guards/interlocks
- follow lifting and material-handling expectations
- keep wet work separated from electrical risks

### 6. Stop and escalate when needed

A Technician I should know the difference between:

**routine correction:** within training and authorization, such as moving a properly movable obstruction out of a walkway.

**stop/escalate:** condition requires maintenance, supervisor, safety lead or emergency response.

Examples:
- damaged energized equipment
- chemical spill outside the technician's trained response scope
- missing required PPE
- worker showing serious heat-illness symptoms
- unsafe ladder/elevated work condition
- suspected gas release
- fire/smoke/electrical fault
- instruction requiring an unauthorized task

## Heat and physically demanding work

Heat risk is affected by:
- air temperature
- humidity
- radiant heat/solar load
- physical workload
- clothing and PPE
- work duration
- acclimatization
- hydration/recovery opportunities

### Technician expectations

- follow the facility's heat plan
- use provided water/recovery periods
- recognize that PPE can increase heat burden
- watch for symptoms in yourself and coworkers
- treat confusion, collapse or major coordination changes as an urgent concern
- do not rely on thirst as the only warning

New or returning workers may require additional acclimatization protections.

## Chemical-task awareness

Technician I chemical responsibilities depend on authorization. At minimum, the technician should understand:
- a product label and SDS are not optional background material
- concentration does not mean “more is better”
- incompatible chemicals should not be mixed casually
- required PPE depends on the task/product
- unlabeled or damaged containers require correction/escalation
- spills must be handled within the person's training and facility procedure
- pesticide application can involve separate legal training/licensing requirements and should never be implied by the Technician I credential alone

## Worked example 1 — Damaged cord near irrigation

**Situation:** A technician sees a cord with visible jacket damage beside a wet irrigation area.

**Weak response:** move the cord six inches and keep using it.

**Better response:** prevent continued routine use, remove the equipment/cord from service according to procedure, control the area if necessary and report the hazard.

**Reasoning:** water and damaged electrical insulation create overlapping hazards. Attention alone is not a sufficient control.

## Worked example 2 — Repeating hose trip hazard

**Situation:** The same irrigation hose crosses an aisle every day.

**Weak response:** put up a small warning note and tell everyone to watch their step.

**Better response:** reroute, bridge, secure or otherwise control the hose under facility procedure so the recurring hazard is physically reduced.

**Reasoning:** source/layout controls are stronger than repeatedly asking workers to remember the hazard.

## Worked example 3 — Hot greenhouse with PPE

**Situation:** Afternoon conditions become hotter and more humid while a task requires protective clothing.

**Weak response:** remove required PPE to stay cooler.

**Better response:** keep required protection and modify the work plan using authorized heat controls such as scheduling, work/rest pattern, water and cool recovery.

**Reasoning:** removing one required control can create a different exposure. The combined risk must be managed.

## Common Technician I mistakes

- continuing with damaged tools because the task is short
- treating PPE as the entire safety program
- stepping over recurring hazards instead of reporting/correcting them
- leaving spills until the end of the shift
- using unapproved improvised repairs
- starting a task when required PPE is unavailable
- assuming experienced workers cannot develop heat illness
- failing to record a hazard after verbally mentioning it
- taking on work outside training/authorization because it seems simple

## Guided practice 1 — Hazard scan

Review a cultivation-room image or simulation. Identify at least eight possible concerns and classify each as:
- routine housekeeping/work-practice issue
- equipment issue
- stop/escalate condition
- needs more information

Then rank the first three actions you would take.

## Guided practice 2 — Stronger control

For each situation, choose a stronger control than “be careful”:
- hose across aisle
- wet floor near a work station
- damaged hand tool
- repeated lifting from floor level
- cord exposed to irrigation traffic

Explain why your chosen control is stronger.

## Guided practice 3 — Heat plan

Given:
- greenhouse conditions
- workload
- shift length
- PPE
- new/experienced worker status

Build a short work plan showing how you would follow the supplied heat procedure and what symptoms would trigger escalation.

## Decision scenarios

### Scenario A — Missing PPE

Required eye protection is not available at the station.

**Best action:** do not begin the controlled task until the required protection or authorized alternative is provided through facility procedure.

### Scenario B — Worker confusion in heat

A coworker becomes confused and unstable during hot work.

**Best action:** stop the work and activate the supplied emergency/heat-illness response rather than waiting for the next break.

### Scenario C — Damaged ladder

A ladder's locking mechanism does not fully engage.

**Best action:** remove it from routine service and use safe approved equipment.

### Scenario D — Small unknown spill

A liquid is found on the floor and its source is unknown.

**Best action:** control access and determine the material/source using facility procedure before assuming it is safe to wipe up.

### Scenario E — Blocked aisle

Materials have narrowed an aisle used for routine traffic/emergency access.

**Best action:** correct or escalate the obstruction according to facility procedure before continuing work that makes the condition worse.

### Scenario F — Unauthorized equipment

A supervisor is unavailable and a technician is asked by a coworker to use powered equipment they have not been trained on.

**Best action:** do not operate it until authorization/training requirements are met.

## Documentation exercise

Complete a mock hazard record containing:
- date/time
- room/area
- hazard description
- immediate control
- equipment/material ID when relevant
- person notified
- assigned corrective action
- current status

Avoid vague entries such as “unsafe room” or “fixed issue.”
